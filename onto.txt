Ontologies, semantic layers and knowledge
graphs
Executive summary
An  ontology (in  computer/information  science)  is  a  formal,  explicit  specification  of  a
conceptualisation that is shared by a community or organisation, typically intended to enable reuse,
interoperability, and automated reasoning. The most-cited foundational definitions emphasise (i) a
shared  vocabulary of concepts and relations and (ii) a  formal (machine-interpretable, logic-based)
specification that supports analysis and computation. 
In practice, ontologies are rarely used alone: they sit in an ecosystem of standards and artefacts. The
W3C Semantic Web stack provides core languages for representing and querying knowledge:  RDF
(graph  data  model),  RDFS (lightweight  schema  vocabulary),  OWL/OWL  2 (ontology  language  with
formal  semantics,  profiles,  and  reasoning  support),  SKOS (model  for  thesauri/taxonomies  and
“knowledge organisation systems”), and SPARQL (query language for RDF graphs). 
Knowledge graphs (KGs), semantic layers, and ontologies overlap but are not interchangeable. A KG
is typically a graph of entities/concepts and typed relations (often with provenance and context) used
for  integration,  search,  analytics,  and  reasoning;  an  ontology  often  acts  as  the  schema/semantic
backbone for a KG (or for data access/integration via mappings). Semantic layers in BI, by contrast,
mainly provide a business-friendly abstraction of data (dimensions, measures, metric definitions), and
may or may not use formal logic; however , ontology-based approaches (e.g., OBDA) can also be viewed
as a semantic layer that enables conceptual querying over heterogeneous sources. 
Recent  NLP  and  LLM  methods  shift  the  construction  of  ontologies  and  KGs  from  purely  manual
engineering  to  hybrid  pipelines:  automated  extraction  (entities/relations),  schema  induction  and
ontology learning, alignment/mapping across sources, and rigorous validation (constraints + reasoning
+ human review). There is a growing research literature characterising how LLMs reshape classical KG
pipelines,  while  also  highlighting  the  need  for  evaluation  and  guardrails  (e.g.,  structured  outputs,
constraint checking, sampling, and audit trails). 
Ontology fundamentals
What an ontology is
A widely cited definition describes an ontology as  a specification of a representational vocabulary
for  a  shared  domain  of  discourse,  including  definitions  of  classes  and  relations.   Another
mainstream  definition  merges  earlier  views  into:  “a  formal,  explicit  specification  of  a  shared
conceptualisation”.   These definitions are compatible: they position an ontology as (a) a  shared
conceptual model and (b) a formal artefact that can be processed by machines. 
In formal terms, many ontologies are grounded in  logic-based knowledge representation (notably
description logics, which underpin OWL profiles and reasoning).   This “logic grounding” is what

Common types of ontology
Ontology “type” is partly about scope and partly about logical strength:
By scope and intent - Upper/top ontologies: very general categories intended for broad reuse across
domains  (e.g.,  notions  like  object,  process,  role).  Upper  ontologies  are  often  used  to  improve
interoperability and alignment across domain models. 
- Domain ontologies: concepts and relations for a specific domain (e.g., finance, manufacturing, clinical
data). 
-  Application/task  ontologies:  tailored  to  a  particular  application  or  task  (e.g.,  decision  support,
configuration), often reflecting operational constraints and required queries. 
By expressivity -  Lightweight ontologies: mostly taxonomic structure and simple constraints (often
close to RDFS/SKOS). 
-  More expressive (heavyweight) ontologies: richer axioms (disjointness, equivalence, restrictions,
property characteristics, etc.) typically in OWL/OWL 2, enabling stronger reasoning. 
Core components and building blocks
Across most ontology engineering practices, these components recur (terminology differs by language,
but the conceptual roles are stable):
Classes (concepts/types) represent sets of individuals (e.g., Person, Company). 
Properties (relations/attributes) relate individuals to individuals (object properties) or individuals to
data values (data properties). 
Individuals/instances are  concrete  entities  in  the  domain  being  described  (e.g.,  “Zurich”,  “Invoice
#123”)—sometimes stored in the same artefact as the ontology, but often separated as instance data
(ABox) in a KG. 
Axioms are  logical  statements  that  constrain  meaning  (e.g.,  subclass,  equivalence,  disjointness,
domain/range) and enable inference. 
Annotations/metadata (labels,  definitions,  provenance)  are  critical  in  practice  for  usability,
governance, and downstream tooling—even when they do not change formal entailments. 
Semantics and reasoning
OWL  2  provides  a  formally  defined  semantics  and  supplies  constructs  for  classes,  properties,
individuals, and data values, with multiple standard documents defining its syntax and interpretation.
 OWL’s  formal  semantics  is  closely  related  to  description  logics;  the  OWL  2  Direct  Semantics
specification explicitly positions it in this lineage. 
A central modelling consequence of logic-based semantics is that many KR formalisms adopt the open-
world assumption: knowledge bases are not assumed complete; absence of a fact does not imply its
negation. This is explicitly discussed in description logic literature and is a key conceptual difference
from many database-style validation mindsets.  For “data quality” style checks (e.g., “every Customer
must have exactly one birth date”), constraint languages such as SHACL define validation over RDF
graphs, complementing OWL reasoning rather than replacing it.

Foundational history and standards
Historical context in brief
Ontology engineering matured at the intersection of knowledge representation, AI, and information
systems. A key early influence in modern CS discourse is Thomas R. Gruber’s 1993 work on portable
ontology specifications, which framed ontologies as shared vocabularies enabling reuse across systems.
 Later  synthesis  work  (e.g.,  “formal,  explicit  specification  of  a  shared  conceptualisation”)
consolidated earlier definitions and became widely adopted in knowledge engineering and Semantic
Web communities. 
The Semantic Web vision popularised ontologies as a web-scale interoperability mechanism. The well-
known Scientific American article by Tim Berners-Lee and co-authors argued for web content with
machine-processable meaning, accelerating standardisation and adoption efforts around RDF/OWL and
linked data approaches. 
Core W3C knowledge representation and querying standards
The following standards are the backbone of many ontology + KG stacks:
RDF (Resource Description Framework) provides a graph-based data model: RDF graphs are sets of
subject–predicate–object triples, and RDF datasets can organise multiple graphs (default + named
graphs).  Historically, RDF became a W3C Recommendation in 1999 and later evolved into RDF 1.1
(Recommendation in 2014). 
RDFS (RDF Schema) adds a lightweight schema vocabulary over RDF, providing modelling primitives
such as class/property hierarchies. The RDFS 1.1 recommendation explicitly describes it as a  data-
modelling vocabulary for RDF data and as an extension of the basic RDF vocabulary. 
OWL / OWL 2 (Web Ontology Language) is a more expressive ontology language on top of RDF with
formally defined semantics and explicit support for classes, properties, individuals, and data values.
OWL became a W3C Recommendation in 2004; OWL 2 became a W3C Recommendation in 2009 (with a
second  edition  in  2012).   OWL  2  also  standardises  profiles (EL/QL/RL)  intended  to  trade  off
expressivity vs reasoning performance. 
SKOS  (Simple  Knowledge  Organization  System) is  designed  to  represent  taxonomies/thesauri/
classification schemes as linked data. The SKOS Reference describes SKOS as a common data model
for sharing and linking knowledge organisation systems via the Web.   SKOS became a W3C
Recommendation in 2009. 
SPARQL 1.1 is the W3C-standard query language for RDF graphs; the SPARQL 1.1 Query specification
defines the syntax and semantics and highlights querying across diverse data sources, including cases
where data is “viewed as RDF via middleware”. 
SHACL (Shapes Constraint Language) is a W3C Recommendation for validating RDF graphs against
constraints  expressed  as  RDF  (“shapes  graphs”),  producing  validation  results/reports.   (Notably,
SHACL continues to evolve; SHACL 1.2 Core appears as a 2026 W3C technical report.)

Conceptual and practical links between ontologies, knowledge
graphs, and semantic layers
Overlaps and differences
Ontologies and knowledge graphs
A practical “stack view” that matches many real deployments is:
An ontology provides shared meaning: vocabularies + constraints/axioms enabling inference. 
A knowledge graph provides instance-level facts (and often provenance/context), typically as a
graph of entities and relations. A comprehensive tutorial paper on KGs describes their data
models, querying, validation, and relationship to schema/ontologies. 
In  this  sense,  ontology  and  KG  are  complements:  the  ontology  is  commonly  the  schema  and
semantics, while the KG is the data (plus potentially a schema). Many KGs are partially schema-less or
use a lightweight schema; others are “ontology-rich” and rely on OWL reasoning. 
Semantic layers (two important meanings)
In enterprise practice, “semantic layer” is used in at least two ways:
1)  BI  semantic  layer  /  semantic  model:  a  business-facing  abstraction  that  defines  dimensions,
relationships,  and  metrics  so  that  downstream  tools  can  issue  consistent  queries  and  compute
consistent KPIs. Looker’s documentation states that LookML is used to create semantic data models
describing dimensions, aggregates/calculations, and relationships, which Looker uses to generate SQL.
 The  dbt  Semantic  Layer  describes  centralised  metric  definitions  for  consistent  downstream
consumption.  Microsoft describes Power BI “semantic models” as data models “ready for reporting
and  visualization”,  reflecting  an  official  interpretation  of  semantic  layer  as  the  modelling  surface
between raw data and reporting. 
2)  Semantic layer for integration/access (ontology-driven): ontology-based data access/integration
(OBDA/OBDI)  treats  an  ontology  +  mappings  as  a  conceptual  layer  that  enables  users  to  query
heterogeneous sources using ontology terms. The OBDA survey and the OBDI definition both frame
this as a semantic paradigm for user-friendly access and integration. 
These two meanings share a goal (usable, consistent semantics) but differ in  formality and typical
reasoning/querying regime: BI semantic layers focus on metrics and business logic; OBDA semantic
layers focus on conceptual interoperability and often DL-based reasoning and query rewriting. 
Roles in data integration, querying, reasoning, and analytics
Data integration - Ontologies support integration by enforcing consistent conceptualisations across
sources, often supplemented by mappings (e.g., OBDA/OBDI) that relate source schemas to ontology
predicates. 
- Knowledge graphs often act as an integration target: extracted/linked facts from multiple systems are
consolidated as a graph with stable identifiers and shared semantics. 
Querying - RDF-centric KGs use SPARQL for querying graphs and federating across endpoints; SPARQL
1.1 explicitly addresses querying across diverse data sources. 
- Property-graph KGs (common in operational graph databases) use graph query languages such as

Automated construction with NLP and LLMs
The classical “text to ontology/KG” pipeline
Ontology learning and KG construction from text are long-standing research areas; the core idea is to
support ontology engineers by extracting candidate terms, relations, and constraints from corpora. 
A widely used decomposition (common across ontology learning literature) includes:
1) Corpus preparation (collect, clean, segment, deduplicate)
2) Term/concept extraction (candidate classes/concepts)
3) Taxonomy induction (subclass relations; concept hierarchies)
4) Relation extraction (non-taxonomic relations)
5) Axiom/constraint learning (domains/ranges, disjointness, cardinalities—harder)
6) Population (instances) and linking (entity resolution, linking across sources)
7) Evaluation and maintenance (iterative refinement) 
Two practical NLP building blocks recur in real systems:
Named Entity Recognition (NER): identifies spans corresponding to entities (people,
organisations, products, etc.). spaCy documents NER as a pipeline component that predicts
labelled spans and notes that performance depends on trained models and may require tuning. 
Open Information Extraction (OpenIE): extracts open-domain relational tuples (subject,
relation, object). Stanford CoreNLP’s OpenIE documentation explicitly describes producing
relation triples such as born-in(Barack Obama, Hawaii). 
These  components  typically  feed  entity  linking (canonical  IDs  for  mentions)  and  relation
normalisation (mapping surface relations like “was born in” to canonical predicates).

Where LLMs change the picture
Recent surveys describe LLMs as shifting KG construction from rigid modular pipelines toward more
unified, language-driven approaches—while still requiring strong normalisation and validation layers.
 A useful lens is the distinction between:
Schema-based LLM pipelines: the ontology/schema is defined (or iteratively refined) and the
LLM extracts facts into that schema; emphasis is on consistency and normalisation. 
Schema-free (or schema-light) LLM pipelines: the LLM proposes entities/relations first, and the
schema emerges from clustering/induction; emphasis is on discovery and flexibility, but with
higher governance risk. 
There is also an emerging benchmarking culture around “LLMs for ontology learning” (e.g., shared tasks
evaluating extraction of taxonomy/non-taxonomic relations and axiom types), reflecting a recognition
that ontology learning is not just about triples but also about axioms and modelling commitments. 
Mapping, alignment, and human-in-the-loop workflows
Mapping  and  alignment are  often  the  hardest  part  of  real  deployments  because  heterogeneous
sources  and  vocabularies  must  be  reconciled.  A  dedicated  body  of  work  treats  this  as  ontology
matching,  aiming  to  find  correspondences  between  ontology  entities  to  resolve  semantic
heterogeneity.   The  Ontology  Alignment  Evaluation  Initiative has  run  evaluation  campaigns
since 2004 and provides shared test cases and evaluation modalities. 
In practice, most production-grade pipelines are human-in-the-loop: - LLM/NLP components generate
candidates (classes, relations, mappings, instance facts). 
- Domain experts validate high-impact elements (core taxonomy, key relations, identity rules). 
- Automated validators (SHACL) + reasoners (OWL profiles) catch inconsistencies early. 
A practical enabler for safer LLM integration is  schema-constrained generation. OpenAI ’s API
documentation describes “Structured Outputs” as enforcing that model outputs adhere to a supplied
JSON Schema, reducing schema-breaking outputs in extraction/population steps.

Notes on interoperability and technology choices
Choosing between RDF triplestores and property-graph databases is often about semantics and tooling
as much as performance. A dedicated paper surveys interoperability issues between RDF and property
graphs and distinguishes syntactic, semantic, and query interoperability challenges.   This matters
because many enterprises end up with both paradigms: RDF for standards-heavy interchange and
reasoning; property graphs for application-centric graph workloads. 
Workflows, validation, and a pilot roadmap
Workflow for building an ontology from scratch
This  workflow  combines  practical  engineering  guidance  (e.g.,  Ontology  Development  101)  with
standards-aware validation:
1)  Scope and purpose definition: define the domain boundary, stakeholders, and intended tasks.
Capture “competency questions” (queries the ontology must support). 
2)  Reuse  review:  survey  existing  vocabularies/ontologies  (including  SKOS  concept  schemes  if  the
domain is mostly taxonomy). Reuse reduces fragmentation. 
3) Conceptual modelling: draft core classes, relations, and key constraints; decide whether you need
OWL expressivity or RDFS/SKOS is sufficient. 
4) Formalisation in OWL (if appropriate): encode classes/properties/axioms using an OWL editor such
as Protégé; decide on OWL 2 profile (EL/QL/RL) if reasoning scalability is a concern. 
5)  IRI/namespace strategy and annotations: establish stable identifiers and human-friendly labels/
definitions. (SKOS-style labels/notes can be useful even for OWL ontologies.) 
6) Reasoning & consistency checks: run a reasoner (profile-appropriate) to detect unsatisfiable classes
and unintended inferences. 
7)  Constraint layer (optional but common): define SHACL shapes for integrity constraints that are
operationally  required  at  ingestion  time  (e.g.,  required  properties,  cardinalities)  and  run  SHACL
validation in CI. 
8)  Release  &  governance:  version  the  ontology;  document  modelling  decisions;  establish  change
control and deprecation patterns. Iteration is expected.

Workflow for extracting a knowledge graph from corpora
A robust corpus-to-KG workflow emphasises repeatability and validation gates:
1) Corpus selection and governance: define document types, licences, and update cadence; create a
“gold” evaluation subset. 
2)  Text  processing  pipeline:  sentence  splitting,  tokenisation,  normalisation;  choose  an  NLP  stack.
spaCy provides configurable processing pipelines; Stanford CoreNLP provides OpenIE. 
3)  Entity extraction: NER + domain-specific dictionaries; optionally fine-tune models for in-domain
entity types. 
4) Relation extraction: OpenIE to get candidate tuples quickly, or supervised relation extraction when
relation inventory is known. 
5)  Entity  resolution  &  linking:  canonicalise  strings  into  stable  IDs;  merge  duplicates;  attach
provenance (document ID, span offsets, confidence). 
6) Schema alignment: map extracted relations to ontology predicates (or induce a schema if starting
schema-free).  Ontology  matching  techniques  and  evaluation  initiatives  (OAEI)  are  relevant  when
aligning to existing ontologies. 
7)  Serialisation and load: represent the KG in RDF (triples/quads) if SPARQL + W3C semantics are
priorities;  consider  property  graphs  for  application-centric  usage.  RDFLib  can  build/query  RDF  in
Python; triplestores/graph DBs handle scale. 
8) Validation: SHACL constraints for ingestion checks; OWL reasoning (when you have an ontology) for
semantic consistency; measure extraction precision/recall on the gold set. 
9)  Query productisation: publish example SPARQL/Cypher queries and dashboards; enforce metric/
definition consistency if the KG feeds BI.

Workflow for using LLMs to assist schema design and population
A safe pattern is to use LLMs for candidate generation and normalisation support, while enforcing
correctness through constraints and review.
1) Provide the modelling contract: give the LLM the ontology (or a schema skeleton) and an explicit
output schema (JSON Schema for extraction results). 
2)  Extract with constrained output: require the LLM to emit only what your pipeline can validate
(entities with IDs, relations drawn from an allowed set, evidence spans). 
3) Post-process deterministically: canonicalise labels; enforce URI policies; reject unknown predicates;
require provenance fields. 
4) Validate: run SHACL (data constraints) and OWL reasoning (semantic constraints) where applicable;
surface violations as feedback to the extraction step. 
5) Human review loops: route low-confidence or high-impact changes (new classes, identity rules, key
mappings) to ontology engineers/domain experts. 
6) Evaluate continuously: maintain a benchmark set of documents and expected triples/axioms; track
extraction metrics and validation error rates release-to-release.

Recommended evaluation checklist and best practices
A compact checklist that mirrors what tends to fail in pilots:
Scope, users, and success criteria - A written set of competency questions and acceptance tests for
priority queries. 
- A decision on whether the primary consumer is search/RAG, integration, BI metrics, or reasoning
(these drive language/tool choices). 
Modelling quality - Clear modelling principles (naming, class/property patterns, identity strategy). 
- OWL profile choice justified by reasoning needs (EL/QL/RL where appropriate). 
Data quality and validation - SHACL shapes for ingestion constraints; CI gate on SHACL validation
reports. 
- Reasoner checks for logical consistency and unintended subsumptions. 
- Provenance captured for edges/nodes (document/source, timestamp, confidence). 
Extraction  and  alignment  quality -  Gold  evaluation  set;  precision/recall  tracked  for  core  entities/
relations. 
-  Alignment  evaluated  (where  relevant)  against  established  methodologies/benchmarks  (OAEI-style
thinking). 
Governance and lifecycle - Versioning of ontology and shapes; deprecation policy; release notes. 
- Human review workflows for schema changes and identity rules.
